# Leadership and Teamwork Questions

Answer templates for questions about leading without formal authority, mentoring, shared decisions, knowledge sharing, and working across teams. Each template shows the structure and the points an interviewer listens for. Fill it with your own experience; if you have not done something, describe how you would approach it and say so.

---

## 1. Tell me about a time you led something without formal authority.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>View Answer</summary>

### Direct answer
Use a story where you pulled people together around a shared goal, without managing them. Show how you built agreement, shared information, and made it easy for others to act.

### Explanation
**Template:**

```text
Situation: [Project or problem], with people from [teams or roles] who did not report to me.
Task:      I took on [the coordinating or deciding part] because [reason].
Action:    I [mapped who needed to agree], [set up a short, regular check-in],
           [wrote down the decision and options for review], and [handled the
           disagreement by ...].
Result:    [Outcome, with a measure]. I learned that [insight about influence].
```

**What interviewers listen for:** how you got buy-in, how you handled a person who resisted, and whether you gave credit to others.

### When to use / pitfalls
- Do not describe "leading" as simply telling people what to do.
- Show how you kept the work moving when the other people had their own priorities.

**See also:** [STAR method](star-method.md)

</details>

## 2. How do you handle a team member who is underperforming?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>View Answer</summary>

### Direct answer
Start with a private conversation to understand the cause, agree on specific expectations and a timeline, check in regularly, and involve your manager or HR when the normal steps are not working. Separate the performance issue from personal circumstances, and treat the person with respect.

### Explanation
**Approach (state it as a process, not as a past event unless it is yours):**

1. Look at the evidence: which tasks, which dates, what was agreed.
2. Talk to the person and ask open questions: "What is getting in the way?"
3. Agree on two or three specific, measurable expectations and a review date.
4. Offer support you can actually give: pairing, clearer tasks, training time.
5. Review progress on schedule, and be direct about what has and has not changed.
6. If it does not improve, involve your manager and follow the organisation's process.

### When to use / pitfalls
- Do not discuss the person with others before you have spoken to them.
- Avoid vague feedback such as "be more proactive". Name the behaviour and the result you need.
- Keep notes, but keep them factual.

**See also:** [Conflict and failure](conflict-and-failure.md)

</details>

## 3. Describe a time you helped a team reach a technical decision.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>View Answer</summary>

### Direct answer
Show that you framed the decision, listed options with trade-offs, gathered input from the people who would live with the result, and helped the group commit to one option with a plan to revisit it. Interviewers look for structure and fairness, not for being the one who was right.

### Explanation
**Template:**

```text
Situation: The team disagreed about [decision, such as a database choice or a service boundary].
Task:      I was asked to help us reach a decision we could all support.
Action:    I wrote a short document with the options, the criteria (cost, risk,
           delivery time), and the trade-offs. I asked each person to state their
           concern. I ran a time-boxed review, and we agreed on [option] with a
           check at [date or measure].
Result:    We [delivered / decided / avoided] [outcome]. I learned that [insight].
```

**Useful phrases:** "What would change your mind?" and "What would we need to see to revisit this?"

### When to use / pitfalls
- Do not present a decision as a win for you. Describe what the group decided.
- If the decision was wrong, say so, and describe how you made the correction.

**See also:** [Conflict and failure](conflict-and-failure.md)

</details>

## 4. How do you share knowledge so the team is not dependent on one person?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>View Answer</summary>

### Direct answer
Make knowledge visible and shared: write short runbooks and decision records, pair on unfamiliar work, run regular code walkthroughs, and rotate ownership of recurring tasks. Measure success by whether others can do the work without you.

### Explanation
**Practices to describe, using your own examples:**

- **Documentation near the code:** a README for each service, a runbook for each operational task, and a short record of important decisions.
- **Pairing and walkthroughs:** a 30-minute session on a new area, with someone doing the typing.
- **Rotation:** swapping on-call or release duties so several people know the process.
- **Code review as teaching:** explaining why, not only what to change.

### When to use / pitfalls
- Documentation that nobody reads is wasted effort. Keep it short and link it from where people look.
- Do not become the only reviewer for everything. Spread reviews across the team.

**See also:** [Career motivation](career-motivation.md)

</details>

## 5. Tell me about a time you mentored someone.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>View Answer</summary>

### Direct answer
Describe a person, their goal, how you found out what they needed, the way you supported them, and what they did with it. Mentoring stories are strongest when you can show the other person's progress, and when you stepped back so they could own the result.

### Explanation
**Template:**

```text
Situation: [Name or role] was [new to a area / preparing for a goal / struggling with X].
Task:      I agreed to support them on [goal], with regular short sessions.
Action:    I asked what they wanted to learn first, gave them a small task with a
           clear outcome, reviewed their work with questions rather than answers,
           and let them make the decision on [specific point].
Result:    They [achieved / took ownership of / presented] [outcome]. I learned that
           [insight about coaching].
```

### When to use / pitfalls
- Do not claim credit for the other person's achievement. Say what you contributed.
- If you have not mentored anyone formally, describe an informal case, such as onboarding a colleague to a codebase, and say so.

**See also:** [STAR method](star-method.md)

</details>

## 6. How do you prioritise when stakeholders all want different things?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>View Answer</summary>

### Direct answer
Make the trade-off visible. List the requests, the value and deadline of each, and the cost of delaying it. Agree on the order with the people who own the priorities, and write the decision down so it can be checked later.

### Explanation
**Approach:**

1. Collect all requests in one place, with the person who asked and why.
2. Ask each stakeholder for the impact if their item is delayed by two weeks.
3. Group items by impact and effort, and identify any hard deadlines.
4. Present the options to the decision owner, not decide alone.
5. Record the agreed order and review it when something changes.

**Template answer:** "I [collected / compared / proposed] [options], showed the trade-off in [unit of value], and [who] agreed on [order]. When [change] happened, we [re-prioritised] by [method]."

### When to use / pitfalls
- Saying yes to everything is a hidden decision that hurts the team later.
- Do not hide the trade-off from the people who lose out. Tell them early.

**See also:** [Conflict and failure](conflict-and-failure.md)

</details>

## 7. What makes a good code review, in your view?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>View Answer</summary>

### Direct answer
A good review checks correctness, clarity, tests, and security, separates must-fix issues from suggestions, explains the reasoning, and keeps the tone respectful. It is quick enough that the author does not wait for days.

### Explanation
**Points to make, in your own words:**

- Review the change, not the author. Ask questions where you are unsure.
- Mark each comment as blocking, a suggestion, or a question.
- Check that tests cover the behaviour, not only the lines.
- Look for security issues: input handling, secrets, and authorisation checks.
- Keep reviews small. Large changes are hard to review well; ask for them to be split.

### When to use / pitfalls
- Style debates belong in a linter or a style guide, not in long review threads.
- Praise good solutions too. It helps people know what to repeat.

**See also:** [Core Java](../technical/core-java-part-1.md)

</details>

## 8. How do you work well with a team in another time zone?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>View Answer</summary>

### Direct answer
Make work visible in writing, agree on overlap hours for decisions, hand off work with clear notes, and record meetings or decisions for those who cannot attend. Plan so that no one waits a full day for a simple answer.

### Explanation
**Practices:**

- Write decisions and open questions in a shared place, with an owner and a due time.
- End each day's handover with what is blocked and what you need.
- Keep a small overlap window for live discussion, and use asynchronous updates for the rest.
- Rotate meeting times when the overlap is not fair to one side.

### When to use / pitfalls
- Asynchronous work fails when updates are vague. "Looking into it" is not a status.
- Be careful that decisions are not made in one time zone and then reversed by a team that was not present.

**See also:** [Career motivation](career-motivation.md)

</details>

## Quick recap

- Leading without authority means building agreement, sharing information, and giving credit.
- Handle underperformance privately, specifically, and with a clear timeline.
- Structure technical decisions around options, criteria, and a revisit point.
- Share knowledge through short documents, pairing, and rotation.
- Make priorities and trade-offs visible, and write the decision down.
