# STAR Method and Answer Structure

The STAR method gives a behavioural answer a clear shape: the situation, your task, the actions you took, and the result. This page explains the structure, offers templates to fill in, and includes one worked example that is invented for illustration. Replace every detail in it with your own experience.

---

## 1. What is the STAR method, and how do you use it?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>View Answer</summary>

### Direct answer
STAR stands for Situation, Task, Action, Result. Spend a sentence or two each on the situation and task, most of the time on the actions you personally took, and finish with a measurable result and what you learned. Aim for about two minutes spoken.

### Explanation
| Part | Purpose | Typical share |
|---|---|---|
| Situation | Set the scene: team, product, and why it mattered | 10-15% |
| Task | Your responsibility, stated clearly | 10% |
| Action | What you did, step by step, using "I" | 50-60% |
| Result | What changed, with numbers if you have them, and what you learned | 15-20% |

**Template:**

```text
Situation: In my role as [your role] at [your context], [the problem or
           situation in one or two sentences].
Task:      I was responsible for [your specific responsibility].
Action:    I [first thing you did], then [second], and [third].
           I chose [approach] because [reason].
Result:    [Measurable outcome]. I also learned [lesson].
```

### When to use / pitfalls
- Say "I" for what you did. Say "we" for what the team did, and be clear about your part.
- Keep the situation short. Interviewers want your actions, not the full background.
- Do not invent metrics. If you did not measure something, describe the change in plain words.

**See also:** [Leadership and teamwork](leadership-and-teamwork.md), [Conflict and failure](conflict-and-failure.md)

</details>

## 2. How do you fill in the Situation and Task without rambling?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>View Answer</summary>

### Direct answer
Name the context in one sentence, the problem in one sentence, and then your responsibility in one sentence. If the interviewer wants more, they will ask.

### Explanation
**Template:**

```text
[Context in one sentence]: "Our [system or team] handled [what it did]."
[Problem in one sentence]: "[What went wrong or what was needed], which affected [who or what]."
[Your task in one sentence]: "I owned [the piece you were responsible for]."
```

**Guidance:**

- Give only the detail the listener needs to understand your actions.
- Avoid internal system names, ticket numbers, or customer names. Describe them generically, such as "a payment service" or "a reporting job".
- Name the stakes: deadline, users affected, or cost. Keep it to one clause.

### When to use / pitfalls
- A long opening spends the time you need for the actions.
- If your task was unclear at the start, say so and say how you clarified it. That is itself a useful action.

**See also:** [STAR method](star-method.md)

</details>

## 3. How do you describe your actions so they show your judgement?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>View Answer</summary>

### Direct answer
Describe the decisions, not just the steps. For each action, say what you considered, what you chose, and why. Interviewers score judgement, trade-offs, and how you worked with others.

### Explanation
**Template:**

```text
I first [investigated / gathered input / measured] to understand [the cause].
I considered [option A] and [option B]. I chose [option] because [trade-off].
I [communicated / involved] [people] by [method], so that [outcome].
I [checked / verified] the result by [method].
```

**Guidance:**

- Use specific verbs: designed, measured, rewrote, negotiated, agreed, rolled out.
- Show the people you involved and how you involved them.
- Include one thing you did that did not go smoothly, and what you did about it.

### When to use / pitfalls
- A list of tasks sounds like a job description. Connect each action to a reason.
- Do not describe actions that someone else took as yours.

**See also:** [Leadership and teamwork](leadership-and-teamwork.md)

</details>

## 4. How do you make the Result strong when you do not have exact numbers?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>View Answer</summary>

### Direct answer
Use a measure you can explain, such as response time, number of incidents, time to complete a task, or the number of people affected. If you have no measurement, describe the change in observable terms and give the feedback you received, without inventing figures.

### Explanation
**Template:**

```text
As a result, [measure] changed from [before] to [after], measured by [how you measured].
If you did not measure it: "The team stopped [the problem]. [Named person or group] told me
that [observable change]."
```

**Guidance:**

- Approximate figures are fine if you say they are approximate. Do not round in a way that changes the meaning.
- Mention what did not work, and what you would do differently. This is part of the result.
- End with the lesson, in one sentence.

### When to use / pitfalls
- Never fabricate a metric. Interviewers often ask how you measured it, and a made-up number falls apart.
- A result that is only "it went well" gives the interviewer nothing to assess.

**See also:** [Conflict and failure](conflict-and-failure.md)

</details>

## 5. Can you see a worked example of the structure?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>View Answer</summary>

### Direct answer
Below is a sample answer. **It is invented for illustration. It is not a real event, and it is not a story to use as your own.** Use it only to see the shape. Write your own version from your own experience.

### Explanation
**SAMPLE - INVENTED - REPLACE WITH YOUR OWN STORY**

> *Situation:* In a team that maintained a nightly batch job, the job had started taking long enough that the morning reports were late, and the team had no clear view of which step was slow.
>
> *Task:* I was asked to find the slow step and bring the job back within its time window.
>
> *Action:* I added timing logs around each step and ran the job against a copy of the data. The logs showed one query did a full table scan. I compared two options: adding an index, or rewriting the query to filter earlier. I chose the index because it kept the change small, and I tested it on the copy first. I agreed the change with the database owner before I released it.
>
> *Result:* The job's run time dropped from the window's limit to well inside it, and the timing logs stayed in place for the next person. I learned to measure before changing anything.

**Why it works:** the situation is short, the task is clear, the actions show a method and a trade-off, and the result is concrete and honest about what was measured.

### When to use / pitfalls
- Do not reuse the invented details (the batch job, the index, the owner) as if they happened to you.
- The structure matters more than the content. Your version needs your real context.

**See also:** [STAR method](star-method.md)

</details>

## 6. How do you build a story bank that covers many questions?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>View Answer</summary>

### Direct answer
Prepare six to eight stories from your own experience, and map each to several themes: leadership, conflict, failure, delivery under pressure, learning, and customer or user impact. One story can answer several questions if you tell it with a different emphasis.

### Explanation
**Template for a story bank entry:**

```text
Story name:     [short label you will remember]
Themes:         [leadership, conflict, failure, ...]
Context:        [one line, no confidential details]
My part:        [what I owned]
Key actions:    [two or three decisions]
Measurable result: [metric, or the observable change]
What I learned: [one sentence]
Follow-ups I can answer: [trade-offs, what I would change, who disagreed]
```

**Guidance:**

- Check each story against what you are allowed to share. Remove confidential names, figures, and internal system names.
- Rehearse out loud, timing yourself. Two minutes is a good target.
- Update the bank after each project so it stays current.

### When to use / pitfalls
- Using the same story for every question makes you look narrow. Keep at least four different ones.
- A story where you were not involved in the decisions will be hard to defend under follow-up questions.

**See also:** [Leadership and teamwork](leadership-and-teamwork.md), [Career motivation](career-motivation.md)

</details>

## 7. How do you handle follow-up questions such as "What would you do differently?"

<details markdown="1">
<summary>View Answer</summary>

### Direct answer
Answer the follow-up directly, in one or two sentences, and show what you learned. Interviewers ask these to test self-awareness and whether your learning is real.

### Explanation
**Template:**

```text
Looking back, I would [change in approach] earlier, specifically [what and when].
I now [habit or practice you adopted], which I have used on [later project] to [outcome].
```

**Common follow-ups and how to prepare:**

- "Who disagreed with you, and how did it end?" Name the position they held, not their personal traits.
- "What were the alternatives?" Be ready to name at least one option you rejected and why.
- "How did you know it worked?" Have one way you checked the result.
- "What would you not do again?" Prepare one honest answer, not a humble-sounding non-answer.

### When to use / pitfalls
- "I would do nothing differently" sounds defensive. Find one real improvement.
- Do not blame people who are not present. Describe the decision and the context.

**See also:** [Conflict and failure](conflict-and-failure.md)

</details>

## 8. What are the most common STAR mistakes?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>View Answer</summary>

### Direct answer
The usual problems are a long background with a short action section, using "we" without saying what you did, no result or a vague one, blaming others, and answers that run past three minutes.

### Explanation
| Mistake | Fix |
|---|---|
| Long setup, rushed actions | Cap the situation at two sentences |
| "We did everything" | Say "I decided", "I built", "I agreed" |
| "It went well" | Give a measure or an observable change |
| Blaming a colleague | Describe the constraint and your response |
| Hypothetical answers presented as experience | Say "in my experience" only when it is true |
| Too many stories | Pick the one that matches the question's theme |

### When to use / pitfalls
- A practice interview with a peer will catch most of these faster than rereading your notes.
- If you cannot find a story for a theme, say so and offer the closest real one.

**See also:** [STAR method](star-method.md)

</details>

---

## Quick recap

- STAR: Situation and Task briefly; Action in detail; Result measured or observable.
- Describe your decisions and trade-offs, not just your steps.
- Never invent metrics or events. Use the sample only to learn the shape.
- Keep six to eight stories from your own experience and rehearse them out loud.
- Prepare an honest answer to "What would you do differently?"
